Field Kit · type and surface

From wireframe to instrument

"Unique but safe and familiar. It currently feels like a wireframe." That is a fair reading, and it is a diagnosable one rather than a taste difference — so this board names the five things that make it read as a wireframe, settles the typeface with real specimens set in the real fonts, and shows the same screen twice so the difference is visible rather than argued.

Why it reads as a wireframe

A border is doing the job of a surface
Every card is the same flat fill with a 1px hairline. That is precisely how a wireframe distinguishes boxes — a rectangle with an outline. Nothing reads as a thing you can touch, because nothing has an edge, a lit top, or a shadow.
Nothing has elevation
Canvas, card and footer are all at the same depth. There is no hierarchy of material — so the eye has only size and colour to go on, and a screen of equal surfaces reads as a layout sketch.
One accent, used only as fill
The amber appears as button backgrounds and almost nothing else — no indicator, no state, no detail. An accent that only fills is a placeholder highlight; an accent that marks states is a system.
Type with no voice
One system face at six sizes, differentiated only by size and weight. That is the default the platform gives you, which is exactly why it reads as undesigned — nothing here was chosen.
Neutrals with nothing in them
The greys are nearly pure. Real instrument surfaces are tinted and slightly metallic, and the tint is what makes adjacent greys read as different materials rather than different opacities.
It is all the same weight of attention
Because every surface is identical, the emergency button is the only thing with contrast — and even that is a flat red rectangle. Hierarchy has to be built from material as well as colour, or the loudest thing is just the brightest.

Readouts are the tell. Etched uppercase labels over mono numerals, on a lit panel — this is where "instrument" stops being a metaphor and becomes a component.

hairline stroke — present, but no longer the shape
lit top edge — the single most effective move
tinted fill, slightly graduated downwards
soft drop shadow — depth, not decoration

That stack is the whole idea. It is applied consistently to every raised surface, at three levels — canvas, panel, control — and it is what stops a rectangle reading as a wireframe box. It is not blur and it is not glass: it is a bevel and a shadow, the way a physical panel catches light. Familiar, because it is how nearly every OS renders a raised surface; unique, because nobody in first aid does it in this palette.

Overpass
recommended		 Descended from Highway Gothic: designed to be read fast, at a distance, in poor light, by someone who is not calm. That is this app's exact reading condition, so the face has a reason rather than a preference. Wide apertures survive a glance. Wider and blunter than a UI grotesque, so it needs slightly tighter tracking and more line-height discipline in body copy.
IBM Plex
Sans + Mono		 The safest of the three and the most "engineered". The mono is genuinely excellent for coordinates and is the smallest of the three to bundle. Very widely used, so it reads a little generic in developer-adjacent products — the opposite of the "unique" half of the brief.
Barlow
+ Semi Condensed		 One superfamily in two widths, which is elegant: condensed for etched labels and numerals, normal for prose. Cheapest to bundle by a factor of two and a half. Reads industrial rather than clinical, and has no signage story — it would be a look rather than a reason.

The system I would set: Overpass for anything a person reads — headings, body, buttons, labels — and IBM Plex Mono only for machine strings: grid references, coordinates, distances, bearings, the metronome rate, the what3words location. Two families, each with one job, and the split is meaningful rather than decorative: words in one face, measured values in the other. That distinction is also the thing that makes the app feel like equipment rather than a document.

What I would do

Bundle Overpass and IBM Plex Mono — about 96 KB of latin subset for four cuts — and apply the three-level surface system above. Those two changes together are what moves it off wireframe: the type gives it a voice, the surfaces give it a body, and neither touches the information architecture we have already agreed.

The "safe and familiar" half is a constraint I would hold deliberately. Nothing about the navigation, the targets, the back behaviour or the tab bar changes. The unfamiliarity should live entirely in material and type — that is what makes it feel designed rather than strange, and it is the difference between a product with a point of view and a redesign.

One thing to decide first: Overpass is the recommendation on the strength of its reason, but it is a signage face and it is wide. If you want the safest option, Plex is the one; if you want the most economical, Barlow. Say which and I will build the token layer around it, then the 999 tab bar on top of that.

How to read this. The three specimens are set in the actual font files, embedded in this page — Overpass, IBM Plex Sans, IBM Plex Mono and Barlow, latin subsets pulled from Google Fonts — so what you are looking at is the typeface, not my description of it. All four are open-licensed and can ship in the app. What this is not: the right-hand frames are a demonstration of material and type at realistic sizes, not a finished screen — the spacing between elements has not been re-tuned for the new weights. The sizes quoted are the downloaded latin-subset sizes, which are what a bundle would actually pay.
