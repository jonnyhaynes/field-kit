References, the 999 tab, and the form

Three things you asked for: what comparable apps actually do, whether the 999 action belongs in the tab bar, and a second pass at the capture form — this time against how long forms are handled elsewhere rather than against my own instinct.

Every number below is quoted from the source rather than estimated, and the sources are listed at the end. Where I have read an app's description rather than used it, I say so.

What comparable apps do

Four categories, because nothing does quite what Field Kit does — offline, cited, and recording rather than advising.

British Red Cross · general first aid

20+ skills, one per scenario

"Simple step-by-step guides", videos and quizzes, and works without internet. A separate Baby & Child app carries a "toolkit" to record a child's medical needs.

Take: a scenario is a destination you pick, not a chapter you scroll to. Offline is stated as a feature, not hidden.
Skip: quizzes and video. They need a library we do not have, and they are learning tools rather than emergency ones.
GoodSAM · responder

Dispatch, kits, and precise location

Alerts responders with the five nearest kits, uses what3words so a caller can say where they are, and streams live caller video to the dispatcher.

Take: "the nearest five, wherever you are" is exactly our AED job; what3words beside a map is the pattern we already have.
Skip: live video and dispatch — it needs a network and a control room, and this app must work with neither.
what3words · the app itself

One thing, done exactly

The whole app is: here is where you are, in three words, and here is how to say them. It works offline by holding the squares locally.

Take: a single dominant answer per screen, and readout type sized for saying aloud. Our "Where I am" already follows it.
Skip: the grid. Their map is the product; ours is one field on a screen that also has to do CPR.
VP0 · first-aid template

The shape most of them share

An emergency list, then one instruction per screen, a CPR rhythm timer, and a call action "present and obvious on every relevant screen".

Take: one instruction per screen is what our CPR screen already does, and their "call on every relevant screen" is the dock rule in someone else's words.
Skip: the teal-and-navy branding those templates all wear.
The pattern across all of them

Emergency apps are shallow and wide

None of them asks a user to work through a long structured form under pressure. The deepest flow is a handful of steps, and the default is a list of situations you pick from.

Take: this is the strongest evidence yet that the responder capture form is the odd one out in our own app — it is the only screen with 47 fields on it.
Where Field Kit differs, deliberately

Cited, offline, and not advising

None of the above reproduces guidance with a named source and review date, none records what an observer saw, and most need a network for their best features.

Keep: the citation line, the offline-first promise, and the refusal to interpret. Those are the identity, and no competitor has them.

What I would not copy: every one of them puts a call action in a large, obvious, full-width element. Ours is the only one considering putting it in a navigation bar, which is the next question.

More
The thing to decide: a tab bar is the most-tapped strip on the screen. An item that dials rather than navigates will be hit by accident far more often than the dock was.
Act Locate Field More 999

The risk, stated plainly. `tel:` opens the dialler with 999 ready — it places no call, so the worst case is a dialler screen you dismiss. That is what makes this acceptable at all. But it is a worse mis-tap surface than the dock.

Why I think you are right
Two stacked bars was the weakest part of the merged direction — I called the bottom-chrome cost "the call I am least sure of", and this removes it. It also kills the inconsistency where Act has no dock and everywhere else does: now there is one bar, one rule.
The one thing to get right
Separate it visually from the navigational items — a divider plus a filled red pill, as drawn — so it reads as an action that lives near the navigation rather than a fifth place. If it looks like a tab, people will tap it to navigate and get the dialler.
A cheaper alternative worth knowing
Keep "Act" as the first tab and make the Act tab itself red. Then the most-tapped item is navigation, the dial still happens on Act behind the OS dialler, and "red means the emergency action" is preserved. It is a smaller change and no new mis-tap surface — but it is two taps to a call instead of one.
What it costs either way
Five items on a 320px bar, and the guidance's own warning about "too many equal calls to action" at the bottom of the screen. The red pill and the divider are what stop it being five equal things.

The capture form, against how long forms are actually done

I went looking for what mature products do with long forms. It partly agrees with my last pass and partly corrects it.

Split only when it earns it — but split at real seams
"Three or four related fields do not need three or four steps… group by meaning, not by arbitrary count: each step should be one coherent idea a person can name." Our four mnemonics are four nameable ideas — SAMPLER, ABCDE, ETHANE, ASHICE — so this supports an index of four rather than a 47-field scroll.
SaaSUI, wizard patterns
The mobile numbers are quite specific
Split past 6–8 questions; 2–5 fields per step; single column; labels above; 44–48px targets — 44×44 measures ~1.6% tap error against 5.8% at 24×24; 8–16px between controls; primary action in a sticky footer.
Reform, mobile multi-step guide
Progress must be honest, and named beats numbered
"A 'Step 3 of 4' where step 4 is the longest form in the flow… teaches people [the bar] cannot be trusted." And named steps beat a bare number because they show what is coming. Our "0 of 21 recorded" is honest but easy to miss and easy to lose.
SaaSUI
Group before you split
The one source that argues first for grouping related fields on one screen and progressive disclosure, and only then for steps. Applied here: ABCDE's A-B-C-D-E is already a grouping — it should be the structure of that step, not 21 loose rows.
UX Design World, long mobile forms
Reveal only what applies
"Display only the fields relevant to the user's prior choices." We already mark fields "Needs equipment you may not have." — eighteen of them. Those belong behind a disclosure, not in the scroll for someone without a cuff or a thermometer.
UX Design World
Back must be lossless, and work must survive interruption
Nothing here should be lost by going back — and a flow this long should save and resume. Our report already persists on the device, so we are most of the way there; nothing in the UI says so.
SaaSUI; Reform

A review step before sending is the other consistent recommendation — gather the answers, let each be corrected, then send. We already have a send screen; it should be doing this job explicitly.

What I would do now

Take the 999 tab bar. Your instinct fixes the weakest part of what we shipped, and the only thing to get right is making it read as an action rather than a fifth destination — a divider and a red pill, not another tab. If the mis-tap worry turns out to bite in testing, the fallback is a red Act tab, which is one tap worse but cannot be hit by accident.

Then the form, and the research makes the case firmer rather than softer. Four nameable ideas, a named stepper inside the longest, equipment fields behind a disclosure, and a review before send. Even the source that argues against splitting forms says to group by meaning first — and our four mnemonics are the grouping, already.

What I would not do yet is redecorate. The references all confirm the visual lane we are in — dark, type-led, one dominant action — and none of them suggests a change that matters more than structure. The type-voice question is the exception, and it still needs answering before more styling.
